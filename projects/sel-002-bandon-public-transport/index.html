01 · Research overview

Research in context.

Bandon is a growing regional town in West Cork with an important relationship to Cork City for commuting, education, healthcare, retail and other services. This study examines how the conditions for using public transport vary across the town rather than treating accessibility as simple proximity to a bus stop.

RQ01

How does public transport accessibility for travel between Bandon and Cork City vary across the town?

The analysis evaluates network-based walking access to principal stops and scheduled peak-period service availability. It describes spatial accessibility and service availability, not a complete comparison of public transport and private-car journey time, cost, reliability or mode choice.

RQ02

Which areas are potentially most vulnerable to limited public transport accessibility?

The study examines where weaker accessibility overlaps with higher relative concentrations of selected age, health, disability, economic-status and household car-availability characteristics.

Study propositionAccessibility ≠ proximity

Transport accessibility depends on network walking access, the scheduled service available after reaching a stop and the population context of the area served. These dimensions are assessed separately before their spatial overlap is examined.

02 · Study area

The geography of the study.

The analytical study area is defined using Census 2022 Built Up Areas. This boundary establishes the consistent spatial extent for the transport-accessibility assessment and the Small Areas included in the population-vulnerability analysis.

Bandon had a Census 2022 population of 8,196, compared with 6,957 in 2016, an increase of approximately 17.8%. Located about 30 km southwest of Cork City, the town functions as a regional settlement and commuter node within West Cork.

The analysis uses Census 2022 Small Areas as the principal reporting geography. Normalisation is calculated across the Small Areas within the defined analytical population, so results describe relative conditions within the Bandon study area rather than the wider hinterland.

04 · Methodology

From data to evidence.

The analytical hierarchy separates transport-system accessibility from population vulnerability. Pedestrian Accessibility and scheduled Service Availability form the Composite Accessibility Index; Population Vulnerability is introduced only at the final accessibility–vulnerability hotspot stage.

01

Census indicators

Build Elderly, Health, No-Car and Economic Vulnerability measures from selected Census 2022 variables.

02

Network walking access

Use ArcGIS Network Analyst service areas for 0–5, 5–10 and 10–15 minute walking bands at 5 km/h.

03

Pedestrian Accessibility

Assign network accessibility scores of 1.0, 0.6, 0.3 and 0.0 to the walking-time classes.

04

Service Availability

Derive scheduled peak-period service frequency and headway from GTFS information.

05

Composite Accessibility

Calculate CAI = 0.50(Pedestrian Accessibility) + 0.50(Service Availability).

06

Population Vulnerability

Calculate 0.30 Elderly + 0.30 Health + 0.20 No-Car + 0.20 Economic after min–max normalisation.

07

Hotspot screening

Calculate Hotspot Score = 0.40 Access Deficit + 0.60 VUL_norm and classify five spatial screening categories.

CAI

Composite Accessibility Index

CAI combines network-based Pedestrian Accessibility and normalised scheduled Service Availability with equal 50:50 weighting. Population Vulnerability is deliberately excluded from the CAI.

HOTSPOT

Accessibility–vulnerability screening

Access Deficit = 1 − CAI_norm. The final Hotspot Score combines Access Deficit at 40% with VUL_norm at 60% to identify areas where weaker accessibility overlaps with higher relative vulnerability.

Walking bands0–5 · 5–10 · 10–15 · >15 min

Intervention Priority is a spatial screening classification — Critical Hotspot, High Priority, Moderate, Low Priority and Well Served — rather than a direct recommendation for capital investment.

06 · Results & research questions

What the evidence shows.

Across the seven-map sequence, the evidence indicates a generally stronger central accessibility area and more variable conditions in peripheral residential areas. Population vulnerability adds a separate dimension that does not follow exactly the same spatial pattern as transport accessibility.

RQ01 · ANSWER

Spatial accessibility for Cork City travel

Public transport accessibility is spatially differentiated across Bandon. Areas within and around the central accessibility corridor generally have shorter network walking journeys to principal stops and stronger scheduled peak-period service availability. Several peripheral residential areas have longer walking journeys and/or weaker scheduled service availability.

The result describes the conditions under which residents can access the Bandon–Cork public transport network. It does not establish competitiveness with private-car travel because journey cost, reliability, parking, congestion, door-to-door time and individual mode choice are not modelled.

RQ02 · ANSWER

Potentially vulnerable areas and groups

Several peripheral neighbourhoods combine relatively weaker accessibility with higher relative Population Vulnerability scores. The model incorporates older age, health and disability, selected economic-status characteristics and households without a car.

These are spatial indicators of potential vulnerability, not direct measurements of individual transport dependence, observed unmet demand or individual disadvantage.

08 · Policy & planning implications

From spatial evidence to action.

Map 01.06 provides a spatial screening framework for identifying locations that warrant further assessment. It does not determine which intervention should be implemented or establish a direct investment case.

Specific areas identified in the report. The final strategic recommendations identify southern and southwestern peripheral neighbourhoods, including Gully, Cloughmacsimon and Kilbrogan, for further investigation of pedestrian connectivity to central bus stops.

09 · Limitations & future research

Interpret the evidence with context.

The results are relative spatial measures produced from specific datasets, geographies, modelling assumptions and weighting choices. They should be read within those limits.

01

Walking assumptions

Network routing assumes a walking speed of 5 km/h and depends on the completeness of the pedestrian network. Individual walking ability, gradients, weather, safety and route preferences are not modelled.

02

GTFS limitations

GTFS represents scheduled timetable information, not real-time vehicle locations, delays, cancellations or observed passenger waiting times.

03

Area-level evidence

Census 2022 Small Areas describe relative spatial conditions and should not be interpreted as individual-level attributes or direct measures of transport dependence.

04

Model assumptions

The 50:50 CAI weighting, 40:60 hotspot weighting and final classification thresholds are analytical assumptions. Alternative choices may produce different spatial results.

Relative normalisation

Min–max normalisation expresses each indicator relative to the observed range within the analytical Bandon population. Scores are therefore relative rather than absolute measures.

Demand and behaviour

The analysis measures potential access to scheduled public transport supply. It does not model observed trip demand, destination accessibility, realised travel behaviour or private-car competitiveness.

Future research priorities

Future work should examine GTFS-Realtime reliability, off-peak/evening/weekend accessibility, access to schools and healthcare, active-travel quality, integration with the Ireland Small Area Quality of Life Index and replication across comparable Irish towns.

Geographic comparability

Census 2022 Small Areas were redrawn for the 2022 Census and should not be assumed to be directly equivalent to earlier Small Area geographies without explicit harmonisation.

10 · Conclusion

A structured view of accessibility.

The Bandon analysis demonstrates how network walking accessibility, scheduled service availability and Census-based population vulnerability can be brought together in a reproducible GIS workflow.

FINDING

Accessibility is spatially differentiated

Transport-system accessibility is generally stronger around the town centre and weaker where network walking journeys to principal stops are longer and/or scheduled peak-period service availability is lower.

CONTRIBUTION

Separate dimensions before integration

The workflow keeps transport-system accessibility and population vulnerability analytically distinct before examining their spatial overlap. This provides a transparent basis for identifying areas that warrant further investigation.

Final interpretationEvidence-led spatial screening

The framework supports further pedestrian, service and flexible-transport investigation. Individual interventions require additional engineering, operational, demand, accessibility, cost and feasibility assessment before implementation.
