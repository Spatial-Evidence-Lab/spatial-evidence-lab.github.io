Executive briefing

Public transport accessibility & equity

Spatial evidence for transport planning, investment screening and repeatable monitoring in Bandon.

Executive summary

Bandon is a regional commuter and gateway node with a strongly centralised public transport accessibility pattern.

Town-centre corridors benefit from close proximity to the four principal bus stops and frequent peak-time services towards Cork City. Peripheral residential estates experience longer network walking distances and weaker service coverage.


Crucially, accessibility and population vulnerability do not follow the same spatial pattern. Lower accessibility can overlap with populations exhibiting greater potential transport dependence, including older adults, persons with disabilities or poor health, long-term unemployed residents and carless households.


The planning implication is therefore equity-led: prioritise locations where physical access deficits coincide with higher population vulnerability.

01 · Research

Two questions. One spatial evidence chain.

The study moves from physical access to effective service accessibility and then to equity-focused screening, keeping transport supply and population vulnerability analytically distinct until the hotspot stage.

RQ01 · Commuter viability

Is public transport a viable alternative to private-car commuting between Bandon and Cork City?

Public transport is assessed through walking-network access and scheduled peak-time service availability.

RQ02 · Transport disadvantage

Which areas are potentially most vulnerable to limited public transport accessibility?

The analysis considers demographic, health, employment and household characteristics that may increase potential dependence on public transport.

02 · Analytical framework

From network access to transport equity

Each stage answers a different analytical question. The final priority model combines access deficit and vulnerability rather than treating vulnerability as accessibility itself.

Evidence principle

Accessibility ≠ vulnerability

Accessibility measures spatial conditions of access to public transport. The vulnerability layer represents population characteristics that may increase potential dependence or exposure to transport disadvantage.

Interpretation

Access → Mobility → Opportunity → Quality of Life

The study places public transport accessibility within a wider spatial evidence framework linking transport conditions to participation in social and economic life.

Decision framework

Diagnose the deficit before choosing the intervention

The hotspot layer identifies where attention is warranted. The deficit framework helps distinguish what type of transport problem should be investigated next.

01
Physical access

Walking deficit

The destination or stop exists, but the pedestrian network produces excessive walking distance or poor connectivity.

Investigate: footpaths · crossings · lighting · links
02
Temporal access

Service deficit

A stop may be physically accessible, but scheduled service frequency is insufficient for practical daily use.

Investigate: frequency · timetable · peak provision
03
Network coverage

Coverage deficit

Residential areas remain outside useful access to the established stop and route network.

Investigate: stops · routes · micro-routing
04
Social significance

Equity priority

Lower accessibility overlaps with higher population vulnerability, increasing the potential significance of the transport deficit.

Investigate: priority areas · site validation

04 · Spatial findings

A clear core-to-periphery pattern

The report identifies a strongly centralised accessibility pattern, with the strongest conditions around the town centre and weaker conditions toward peripheral residential areas.

Finding 01

Walking access declines outward

Areas closer to the established stop network have stronger accessibility; longer network walking distances contribute to weaker conditions in peripheral locations.

Finding 02

Proximity is not service utility

A stop can be physically close while effective accessibility remains weaker where scheduled service is infrequent.

Finding 03

Need and supply do not always coincide

Some peripheral residential areas combine lower accessibility with characteristics associated with greater potential transport dependence.

How to read the hotspot result: Critical and High Priority locations are screening evidence. They do not, by themselves, establish unmet demand or determine a specific investment decision.
05 · Method

Reproducible analytical construction

The analytical stages are separated so that transport supply, physical accessibility and population vulnerability remain transparent throughout the evidence chain.

Walking accessibility

Network-based, not straight-line

Service areas are generated around stops at 5, 10 and 15-minute thresholds. The precise walking-speed parameter should remain retained in processing metadata.

Service frequency

Scheduled temporal availability

GTFS trips and stop times are processed to estimate peak-time service frequency. Exact peak windows and the frequency calculation should remain documented in the technical record.

Vulnerability

Four population-need domains

Demographic vulnerability, health and mobility vulnerability, socio-economic vulnerability and transport dependence are represented using selected Census 2022 indicators.

Composite Accessibility Index

Relative spatial index

The CAI integrates walking access and service frequency and is classified as Very Poor, Poor, Moderate, Good and Excellent.

Hotspot ScoreEquity = 0.4 × Access Deficit + 0.6 × VULnorm

Access Deficit = 1 − Normalised CAI. The primary model gives greater weight to population vulnerability; a 50/50 balanced benchmark is also documented in the report.

Published map outputs should always be interpretable through their source data, spatial unit, transformation and analytical role. Where a processing parameter is not yet exposed on this page, it should be retained in the project technical record rather than inferred from the map.

Relative scores identify spatial differences within the study area. They should not be interpreted as absolute measures of accessibility, unmet demand, service quality or investment benefit.

07 · Planning implications

Evidence for targeted investigation

The recommendations are framed as spatial screening and monitoring actions, not automatic investment decisions.

01

Priority infrastructure interventions

Use Map 01.06 as an initial screening layer. Investigate Critical Hotspot areas for footpath connectivity, lighting and safer crossings along access corridors to central stops.

02

Bus stop expansion and micro-routing

Evaluate peripheral areas for additional stops, feeder arrangements or local loop routing where demand and operational feasibility support intervention.

03

Peak service frequency optimisation

Use the service-frequency analysis to examine whether peak-period service levels adequately support movement between Bandon and Cork City.

04

Institutionalise GIS accessibility monitoring

Rerun the workflow as updated GTFS, Census and network data become available, creating a repeatable evidence base for monitoring change.

08 · Limitations & next research

What the analysis does — and does not — measure

These constraints are part of the interpretation framework and should remain visible alongside the published maps.

GTFS

Scheduled, not real-time

GTFS does not necessarily capture delays, cancellations, diversions, crowding or other operational conditions.

Demand

Accessibility is not ridership

The index does not measure actual ridership, trip demand, individual behaviour or unmet demand.

Census

2022 population reference

Vulnerability represents the Census 2022 population reference rather than a continuously updated population estimate.

Index interpretation

Component maps still matter

Composite indices simplify several dimensions into a single score or class and should be interpreted alongside the underlying maps.

Future

Time-of-day expansion

Extend the service analysis beyond peak periods to morning, inter-peak, evening and weekend accessibility.

Future

Replication

The methodology can be tested across other Irish towns, including Clonakilty, Skibbereen, Bantry, Midleton and Mallow.

10 · Conclusion

From access to equity

The Bandon analysis demonstrates that public transport accessibility cannot be understood from stop proximity alone. The spatial pattern is shaped by the interaction between the transport network, walking connectivity, scheduled service frequency and the characteristics of the population living in different locations.

The evidence indicates a broad central-to-peripheral accessibility pattern, while the equity analysis shows why areas with poor accessibility are not necessarily the same as areas where poor accessibility may have the greatest social significance.

The resulting planning logic is therefore simple: maintain strong-performing areas, target identifiable walking, service and coverage deficits, and prioritise locations where lower access overlaps with greater population vulnerability.

Future development can extend the evidence through real-time service data, destination-specific accessibility, active-travel quality, observed travel behaviour, demand modelling and replication across Irish towns.
