01.05 — Effective Accessibility

The composite result moves beyond simple proximity by combining walking access with scheduled public transport service.

KEY RESULT · MAP 01.05

Composite Public Transport Accessibility Index

The Composite Accessibility Index (CAI) integrates walking-network accessibility with scheduled public transport service frequency. This provides a combined measure of effective public transport accessibility across Bandon.

KEY FINDING Accessibility is generally strongest within and around the central urban area, while lower composite accessibility extends toward peripheral residential areas.
INTERPRETATION Proximity to a public transport stop does not necessarily indicate equivalent effective accessibility. Walking access and scheduled service frequency both influence the overall accessibility result.
PLANNING RELEVANCE Areas with lower composite accessibility provide a screening layer for investigating pedestrian connections, stop provision and public transport service coverage.
05 · Evidence Synthesis

What the Evidence Shows

The seven maps should be interpreted as a connected evidence chain: network structure → walking access → service availability → vulnerability → composite accessibility → equity priority.

01 · NETWORK → CONNECTIVITY

Public transport access is structured by the existing network

The transport network establishes the basic geography of accessibility. Locations close to stops and connected walking routes have greater potential access, while areas beyond the practical reach of the network are more spatially constrained.

02 · WALKING → CORE–PERIPHERY

Walking accessibility generally weakens toward the periphery

The walking catchments and accessibility score indicate a broad core-to-periphery pattern. Central areas benefit from closer access to the transport network, while peripheral residential locations tend to experience greater walking distances or weaker network connectivity.

03 · SERVICE → FREQUENCY

A nearby stop does not guarantee equivalent service access

Peak-time service introduces an additional dimension to accessibility. Two locations may have similar walking access to a stop while experiencing different levels of scheduled service. Effective accessibility therefore depends on both physical access and service availability.

04 · VULNERABILITY → EXPOSURE

Population vulnerability is spatially differentiated

Vulnerability is not distributed uniformly across Bandon. Areas with greater potential transport dependence therefore need to be considered alongside the accessibility pattern rather than assessed independently.

05 · EQUITY → PRIORITY

The strongest equity signal occurs where deficits overlap with vulnerability

The final hotspot analysis brings the evidence together. Locations with lower accessibility and higher vulnerability receive greater screening priority, shifting the analysis from simply asking where access is poor to asking where poor access may matter most.

Evidence chain

01.00 establishes the network → 01.01 measures walking reach → 01.02 quantifies walking accessibility → 01.03 adds service frequency → 01.04 identifies vulnerability → 01.05 produces the composite accessibility result → 01.06 identifies potential equity hotspots.

Spatial Interpretation

Spatial Typology

The combined evidence suggests three broad spatial conditions that can be used to interpret the accessibility pattern.

Type 01

Well-Served Core

Central urban areas where walking access and public transport service are comparatively strong.

  • Shorter walking distances
  • Greater network connectivity
  • Stronger composite accessibility
Type 02

Transitional Residential Areas

Areas where accessibility remains available but becomes more dependent on walking connections and service characteristics.

  • Intermediate walking access
  • Variable service availability
  • Potential localised accessibility deficits
Type 03

Peripheral Estates & Margins

Outer residential locations where walking access, service availability and vulnerability may combine to create greater potential transport disadvantage.

  • Longer walking access
  • Lower composite accessibility
  • Potential hotspot conditions

07 · Planning

Planning Implications

The analysis provides a spatial screening framework for identifying where further transport and public-realm investigation may provide the greatest potential benefit.

01

Priority infrastructure interventions

Use Map 01.06 as an initial screening layer for potential investment in pedestrian infrastructure. Critical hotspot areas can be investigated for footpath connectivity, lighting and safer crossings along access corridors to central public transport stops.

02

Bus stop network expansion and micro-routing

Areas with weaker walking access can be evaluated for additional stops, feeder arrangements or local loop routing. Peripheral residential areas may benefit from closer connections to the existing network where demand and operational feasibility support intervention.

03

Peak service frequency optimisation

The service-frequency analysis provides a basis for examining whether peak-period service levels adequately support movement between Bandon and Cork City and whether identified accessibility deficits are partly associated with service availability.

04

Institutionalise GIS accessibility monitoring

The workflow can be incorporated into local planning and transport monitoring processes and rerun as updated GTFS, Census and network data become available.

Conclusion

From Access to Equity

The Bandon analysis demonstrates that public transport accessibility cannot be understood from stop proximity alone. The spatial pattern is shaped by the interaction between the transport network, walking connectivity, scheduled service frequency and the characteristics of the population living in different locations.

The evidence indicates a broad central-to-peripheral accessibility pattern, with the strongest composite accessibility generally associated with the central urban area and lower accessibility extending into peripheral residential locations.

The final equity analysis adds an important planning dimension: areas with poor accessibility are not necessarily the same as areas where poor accessibility may have the greatest social significance. Combining accessibility deficits with vulnerability provides a more targeted basis for screening potential transport disadvantage.

Next research

Future development could extend the analysis through more detailed temporal service analysis, population-demand modelling, accessibility by destination type, validation against observed travel behaviour and scenario testing for proposed stop, routing or service interventions.
