From accessibility to transport equity

EQUITY RESULT · MAP 01.06

Accessibility–Vulnerability Hotspots

Key finding

Priority areas emerge where accessibility deficits and population vulnerability occur together, rather than where either condition exists in isolation.

03 · Evidence

What the evidence shows

The maps form a connected evidence chain rather than seven independent outputs.

NETWORK

Access is structured by the existing network

Locations close to stops and connected walking routes have greater potential access, while areas beyond the practical reach of the network are more spatially constrained.

WALKING

Accessibility generally weakens toward the periphery

Central areas benefit from closer access to the transport network, while peripheral residential locations tend to experience greater walking distances or weaker connectivity.

SERVICE

A nearby stop does not guarantee equivalent service

Peak-time service adds another dimension to accessibility. Locations with similar walking access may experience different scheduled service.

VULNERABILITY

Population vulnerability is spatially differentiated

Vulnerability is not distributed uniformly. Accessibility therefore needs to be considered alongside the characteristics of the population potentially affected by poor access.

EQUITY

The strongest signal occurs where deficits overlap with vulnerability

The hotspot analysis shifts the question from where access is poor to where poor access may have greater social significance.

Evidence chain: 01.00 network → 01.01 walking reach → 01.02 walking accessibility → 01.03 service frequency → 01.04 vulnerability → 01.05 composite accessibility → 01.06 equity hotspots.
04 · Method

How the analysis was built

The workflow separates network accessibility, scheduled service, population vulnerability and equity screening.

The analysis uses practical walking access rather than straight-line proximity, combines this with scheduled service information, and then introduces population vulnerability to create an equity-focused screening layer.

01.01 Generate walking-time catchments
01.02 Derive walking accessibility
01.03 Measure peak-time service
01.04 → 01.05 Add vulnerability and composite accessibility

Processing

Spatial Integration

Transport, walking, service and population datasets are processed through sequential GIS operations to generate comparable spatial measures.

Analytical Integration

Composite Accessibility & Equity Screening

Walking accessibility and scheduled service frequency are combined to produce the Composite Accessibility Index. The composite result is then used to derive an accessibility deficit, which is combined with normalised population vulnerability for the final hotspot screening.

Hotspot screening model
Hotspot Score = 0.40 × Access Deficit + 0.60 × VULnorm
Higher values indicate greater combined screening priority based on accessibility deficit and population vulnerability.

06 · Planning

Planning implications

The analysis is a screening framework for identifying where further transport and public-realm investigation may provide the greatest potential benefit.

01

Prioritise pedestrian infrastructure investigation

Use Map 01.06 as an initial screening layer for footpath connectivity, crossings, lighting and safer walking corridors to public transport.

02

Investigate stop coverage and local connections

Areas with weaker walking access can be evaluated for additional stops, feeder arrangements or local routing where demand and operational feasibility support intervention.

03

Examine peak-period service levels

Service-frequency results provide a basis for investigating whether peak-period service is adequately aligned with identified accessibility deficits.

04

Establish repeatable accessibility monitoring

The workflow can be rerun as updated GTFS, Census and network data become available, allowing accessibility conditions to be monitored over time.

Conclusion

From access to equity

Public transport accessibility cannot be understood from stop proximity alone. The spatial pattern is shaped by the interaction between the transport network, walking connectivity, scheduled service frequency and population characteristics.

The evidence indicates a broad central-to-peripheral accessibility pattern, with stronger composite accessibility generally associated with the central urban area and lower accessibility extending toward peripheral residential locations.

The final equity analysis adds a planning dimension: areas with poor accessibility are not necessarily the same areas where poor accessibility may have the greatest social significance. Combining accessibility deficits with vulnerability provides a more targeted basis for screening potential transport disadvantage.

Interpretation & limitation

The outputs are a spatial screening framework, not a final investment decision. Results should be interpreted alongside local knowledge, current service conditions, site assessment, operational feasibility and stakeholder engagement.

Next research

Future development could extend the analysis through detailed temporal service analysis, population-demand modelling, destination-based accessibility, observed travel behaviour and scenario testing for proposed stops, routes or service interventions.
